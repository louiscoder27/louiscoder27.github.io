Beginners obsess over *what* to buy. Experienced investors obsess over *how much*. After years of watching my own results, I'm convinced position sizing — not stock picking — is where most individual investors quietly win or lose.

## Being right isn't enough

You can be right about a company and still lose money, if you sized the position so large that an ordinary drawdown forced you to sell at the bottom. And you can be right and barely benefit, if the position was so small it didn't move your portfolio.

The goal of sizing is to **stay in the game long enough for your thesis to play out.** A position you can't hold through a 30% drop isn't an investment; it's a countdown timer.

## A simple framework I actually use

I don't run a fancy model. I ask three questions:

1. **Conviction** — how well do I understand this, on a scale I'm honest about? Low conviction, small size.
2. **Correlation** — does this move with things I already own? Five bank stocks isn't five positions; it's one bet on banks.
3. **Ruin** — if this went to zero tomorrow, would it change my life? If yes, it's too big.

## The math that keeps me humble

A position down 50% needs a 100% gain just to break even. That asymmetry is the whole argument for restraint. Sizing so that no single mistake is fatal isn't timidity — it's what lets you keep making decisions after a bad one.

The unglamorous truth: the investors I admire aren't the ones with the best ideas. They're the ones who were still around to use them.
