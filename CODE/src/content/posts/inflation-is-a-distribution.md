When the inflation print lands each month, it gets reported as a single number. That number is an average — and averages hide the thing that actually affects people: **which prices moved, and for whom.**

## The average conceals the spread

Say inflation is 3%. That could mean almost every price rose about 3%, or it could mean rents rose 8%, energy fell 5%, and services quietly climbed while goods deflated. The average is identical; the lived experience is not. A household that rents and drives feels a very different 3% than one that owns outright and works from home.

For an investor, the composition matters because different drivers call for different responses:

- **Goods inflation** often reflects supply chains and tends to be transitory.
- **Services inflation** is stickier — it's mostly wages, and wages don't fall easily.
- **Shelter** moves slowly and lags real-time rents, which distorts the headline for months.

## Why this changes how I read the data

A central bank looking at 3% driven by one-off energy spikes will act very differently from one seeing 3% driven by broad services. So when I read an inflation report, I skip the headline first and go to the breakdown:

1. Is the pressure **broadening** across categories, or narrowing to a few?
2. Is it in **sticky** components or **flexible** ones?
3. What's the trend once you strip out the slow-moving shelter lag?

The single number makes a good headline. The distribution behind it is where the actual signal lives — and it's usually telling a more nuanced story than the panic or the relief the headline invites.
