Most people start with the income statement. I've learned to start at the back, with the cash flow statement, because it answers the one question the other two only imply: **did money actually come in?**

## Three sections, one story

The statement splits a year of cash into three buckets — operating, investing, and financing. Read together, they tell you how a business funds itself.

- **Operating** — cash from the core business. This is the engine.
- **Investing** — what the company spent to grow or maintain itself (capital expenditure), plus acquisitions and asset sales.
- **Financing** — money raised from or returned to lenders and shareholders.

A healthy, self-funding company generates more from operations than it spends on investing, and uses the difference to pay down debt or return cash. A company that funds its dividend from the financing section is quietly borrowing to look generous.

## Free cash flow is the number that matters

> Free cash flow = operating cash flow − capital expenditure.

It's the cash left over after keeping the lights on and the machines running. Everything a company can *actually* do — pay dividends, buy back stock, repay debt, acquire a rival — comes out of this pool. If reported earnings keep rising while free cash flow stagnates, something in the accruals deserves a second look.

## Where the bodies are buried

A few patterns worth a raised eyebrow:

1. **Earnings up, operating cash flow flat.** Often a sign that profit is being booked before the cash arrives — watch receivables.
2. **"One-time" items that recur every year.** If it happens annually, it isn't one-time.
3. **Capitalising costs that look like expenses.** It flatters profit today and haunts cash flow later.

None of these are proof of anything on their own. They're questions, not verdicts. But the cash flow statement is where you learn which questions to ask.
