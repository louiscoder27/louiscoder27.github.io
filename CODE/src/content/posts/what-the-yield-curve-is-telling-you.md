Every few months a chart of the yield curve goes viral with a caption about the next recession. The signal is real — but it's badly explained. The curve isn't a crystal ball; it's a **poll of the bond market's expectations**, and knowing what it's voting on makes it far more useful.

## What the curve is

Plot the interest rate on government bonds against how long until they mature — 3 months, 2 years, 10 years — and you get a line. Normally it slopes up: lenders want more yield to tie their money up for longer.

When short-term rates rise *above* long-term rates, the curve **inverts**. That's the part everyone screenshots.

## Why inversion happens

An inverted curve is the market saying two things at once:

- **Short rates are high** because the central bank is fighting inflation now.
- **Long rates are lower** because investors expect those high rates — and the growth behind them — to fade later.

In other words, the bond market is pricing in *future cuts*. Inversion is a bet that today's tight policy will slow the economy enough that rates have to come down.

## The part the headlines skip

Two caveats keep me honest here:

1. **The lag is long and variable.** Historically, inversions have preceded recessions — but by anywhere from six months to two years. As a market-timing tool, that's close to useless.
2. **It's a signal, not a mechanism.** The curve doesn't *cause* a slowdown; it reflects one that participants already suspect.

So I treat inversion the way I'd treat a barometer falling: worth noting, worth preparing for, not worth panic. The useful move isn't to sell everything — it's to check whether my portfolio could survive the scenario the curve is quietly pricing in.
