"Moat" might be the most overused word in investing. Companies claim one the way restaurants claim to be authentic. A real economic moat is specific and testable: it's a **structural reason a competitor can't simply copy the business and compete the profit away.**

## Four moats that tend to hold

Most durable advantages fall into a handful of shapes:

- **Network effects** — the product gets more useful as more people use it. Hard to dislodge because value lives in the users, not the code.
- **Switching costs** — leaving is painful, expensive, or risky. Enterprise software lives here.
- **Cost advantage** — the company can profitably undercut everyone, usually via scale or a unique asset.
- **Intangibles** — brands, patents, or licences that competitors legally or psychologically can't replicate.

## The test I apply

For any claimed moat, I ask one question: *if a well-funded, competent rival tried to take this business, what specifically stops them?* If the honest answer is "nothing but our head start," it isn't a moat — it's momentum. Momentum is worth something, but it's priced very differently.

## What a moat looks like in the numbers

Moats leave fingerprints on the financials:

1. **Stable or rising margins** over a full cycle, not just a boom.
2. **High returns on invested capital** that *persist* — the market's job is to compete those away, so persistence is the tell.
3. **Pricing power** — the ability to raise prices without losing volume.

A story about the future is easy to write. A decade of returns on capital that refused to mean-revert is much harder to fake — and much more interesting.
